## "Starting With AI: What Actually Works in 2026," with Michael Simmons

Host: Michael Simmons, creator of Blueprint Newsletter and CoZora.org.
Alex introduced as: creator of GPT Central (AI Central), from Italy, based
in London, over 250,000 subscribers, MSc in quantitative finance, taught
applied AI in finance academically, written for industry publications.

**Michael:** So I'm curious, how do you look at the future of the media
business now that AI is increasingly getting involved in researching
things, synthesizing them and creating content? Do we have a place?

**Alex:** Well, that's a very interesting question... I think that the
answer is probably there will be opportunity for everyone. So it's a
market, the creator economy, that is getting bigger and bigger by the
years... there is an imbalance between supply and demand from this point
of view. Meaning, yes, there is a growing number of media operators,
newsletters, creators, but also an increasing number, on a higher order of
magnitude, of people that are joining the market for content, for courses,
for information that is alternative to the traditional media channels...
The channels are different, the media are different, but the opportunity
for creators right now is to be simultaneously omnichannel and being able
to follow your reader's journey.

Clearly, you mentioned AI. AI is the one, if not the only, century-long -
or in this case, decade-wide - transformation. Finally we are seeing now
the fruits of AI delivering, after maybe a century of people trying a
million different approaches to making it deliver. And AI is creating
value for people with strong fundamentals.

**Michael:** What do you see as the most promising [monetization] for
creators to pay attention to that almost no one else is talking about?

**Alex:** That's also a question where it's hard for a media business
entrepreneur to have a clear-cut answer. Monetization is so highly
dependent on a lot of other factors in your business equation. You have
your editorial model, your growth model, your audience model - who you're
talking to - as well as your stack model, how many people are in your
company. My take is that the monetization stack you end up having is more
the result of all these other factors than the other way around. You
don't start with the monetization strategy. Monetization strategy is the
result of who you're talking to, how you're growing, how you deliver
content, how frequently you deliver content, how your digital real estate
is worth it and expanding.

[On individual creators vs a newspaper's monetization] ...on the other
spectrum, if you take individual creators, which are typically either
solopreneurs or with a very tight team, the monetization follows way more
some logic of - hey, let me try to have some monetization events in the
lifetime of my subscriber - instead of necessarily going with volume.

**Michael:** How do you feel like you view [the Substack creator] journey
differently than other people?

**Alex:** I think that Substack is a platform which has been growing over
the years... it's a subscription-driven platform in that sense, which made
a lot of sense in the early days where you could tier your content and the
gating of the content could be basically unlocked through a subscription.
A subscription thought as a club membership, to a certain degree.

In the meantime, of that newsletter industry growing tenfold over the last
three, four, five years, the monetization opportunities for newsletter
operators expanded a hundredfold. So yes, maybe the most easy to
understand is the subscription, but there are a lot of other options -
selling digital products, courses, coaching, access to specific tools...
And not necessarily has Substack gone into that direction. And that is, to
a certain degree, a deliberate choice - I don't think there is anything to
complain about. It's product strategy.

I am a client, an enterprise client of beehiiv, and a big advocate of the
platform, to be fair. beehiiv is a platform that is built as an operating
system for newsletter operators. I hate the discussion of who's the best
platform. I think it depends on: are you a creator, a content-first
person? Then you have a business model. Are you a newsletter operator or a
media business? Then you have way other struggles - deliverability,
audience growth, paid ads, sign-up flow survey, first-party data strategy,
the website, then your monetization. I think one big challenge for anyone
in this industry is having the leanest, most sleek, most efficient stack
possible, because operations increase in complexity as you grow and you
don't want your stack to hold you back from growth.

**Michael:** Would you recommend, when would you recommend someone just
stay with one platform vs. going wide like you have?

**Alex:** To be completely honest, my story is I started on Substack, then
I moved to beehiiv, while having also my Substack on the side. The reason
for me to change from Substack to beehiiv was mostly that the implied cost
of running a Substack - since my first monetization strategy was
subscription - at a certain point I realized that I was paying Substack
$500 a month. Substack has an implied cost that is fair in the creator
business - it's a fair cut. But the moment you start with subscriptions
and then you scale subscriptions, it implicitly becomes a cost when you do
your balance sheet. Switching to beehiiv was the choice that made more
economical sense from a business perspective, since the creator economy at
a certain point ends up with you creating value, getting some revenues,
and then having all these other tools eating with fees on your revenues,
and you end up with peanuts. Keeping all your revenue was essential in the
early days to reinvest into the growth, into hiring people, into growing
the system.

Then over time, I kept building on beehiiv. It made a lot of sense -
automation, website... the platform kept shipping features every two
weeks, the users were really put at the front. You could see the founder
of beehiiv having built his own newsletter and now he's in a million in
revenues on that - that was also a very inspiring story.

It's an ill-posed question, what's the best [platform]. Substack has a
top-tier discoverability engine... they lack on some features that beehiiv
has for growing the business - monetization, building segments, list
cleanup, deliverability, building websites. The two things can combine.
It's like asking, is it better New York or San Francisco? Maybe San
Francisco is better for some things, New York is better for other things.
How about we put two and two together and use each platform for what's
best.

With all the other platforms, I grew mostly 90%, 95% of my audience from
LinkedIn. Because that's where my audience comes - professional, over 45
roughly, where I teach them how to use AI and consult them in some cases.
What everyone should take home, and that I take home from my experience,
is the value of owning your readership, owning your subscriber base -
because this is the real real estate, not being tied to a platform. There
are plenty of them, there is Ghost, I could mention another 10. But what
happens the day you grow and you want to change platform, migrate your
subscriptions? Some platforms have incentives for locking you in. Some
other platforms just want to give you the tools to grow your business.
This is where the real battle is happening.

**Michael:** [on data] What do you feel like are the most important data
[for] an AI media company?

**Alex:** First of all, let me do a distinction. There are no AI media
companies. There are media companies with AI. So AI-first media companies
- a faceless channel with an avatar talking on 30 platforms - this is not
what we are dealing with. What we want to deal with is a person that has a
background, that has some expertise, that is packaging this expertise into
content, distributing it on multiple platforms - where AI can help this
person, a human being talking to other human beings.

I think the biggest exercise anyone should be doing is, first of all,
audit which data any platform is exposing... open rates and click-through
rates somehow are inflated as numbers, because algorithms do open
newsletters. A lot of people do not do deep links or shortening of links,
so they don't have full visibility. If you don't know that, you live in a
guess world instead of a science world - which is fine at the beginning,
you should do this job because you like it, you love it, you want to
engage with people. But at a certain point you also ask yourself, how much
time am I spending doing this? For example, the first immediate win I had
for a lot of my clients has been - let's use link shortening, so if we
want to change the landing page, we just go into the link management
platform and change it, instead of entering 50 posts. Link shortening also
gives you the possibility of retargeting.

This is where AI can help you the most - and then at a certain point you
ask yourself, how do I put my growth on repeat, how do I reduce churn, how
do I manage the unsubscribe flow. One of the things that separates
Substack from beehiiv is that beehiiv has the strongest ever preference
center for your subscribers - maybe some people don't want to receive your
emails for a month because it's holiday time, they want to pause. On
Substack, most of the time the only option is unsubscribe.

So there is this graduation ladder. You start with something free, it's
there, everyone knows that, you can do your things, then you graduate to
another level, and it's just natural - it doesn't feel like a battle. You
don't say to someone playing soccer, buy the most expensive shoes. You say
just buy shoes, you can also play soccer with sneakers. Then maybe you
start being a little more serious, you buy entry-level shoes. Then maybe
you go playing in the Premier League and you say, maybe I should have
custom-made shoes. It's just a natural evolution.

**Michael:** Is there a type of monetization you think is good for
creators and is overlooked, beyond subscriptions and advertising?

**Alex:** I would say not necessarily. This comes from my experience
working either with other newsletter operators or with consulting
clients, helping them monetize. The real question is not what are the
monetization strategies that are overlooked, but what are your clients not
getting enough of? [Example: a go-to-market creator with 20,000
subscribers, 5-10% of whom are CMOs/sales directors with near-infinite
price elasticity - their problem is comparing, not price. For them, a
cheat sheet, a map, a consulting call - digital product is a big umbrella
world under which anything can be a digital product. A PDF, a link, a
Google Doc, an unlisted video walkthrough.] Digital product is anything
that your audience is not finding an answer to, and that would take them
hours - dozens of hours - to get a solution of, and you just do the
research for them. What people are paying today is time. We are having a
supply of tools going to infinite, a supply of content going to infinite,
but your time is still 24 hours.

[On what content works right now] One trend my team and I are noticing:
maybe one year ago versus today, tens of millions more people have
experienced their first touch with AI. So there are newcomers, and today,
compared to one or two years ago, they can find someone that has put
things in order for them. There is a big vocabulary problem - connectors,
what's MCP, memories vs custom instructions, projects vs GPTs. So one type
of content that's working extremely well is operating manuals, handbooks -
how to set up your ChatGPT, how memories are different from custom
instructions. The second type of content is comparisons - once you have
your first thousand interactions with AI, your question becomes, but I
also heard someone using Claude, I heard someone using Gemini, at work
they tell me use Copilot because they bought a six-figure license. Is it
really for the average user, the camera of the iPhone doing a different
job from the camera of the latest flagship Samsung? For the average
person, it's unnoticeable. For the pro, it makes all the difference. But
for the average person, they do their job.

## "Demo to Dependable," with Diraj (Gen AI Unplugged)

Host: Diraj, creator of "Gen AI Unplugged," background in Cloud FinOps at
an IT services firm, ~17 years in content/travel-blog work before AI.
Recorded as one of the first published interviews on AI Central's own
"AI Central Voices" / thecentral.ai/interviews page - Alex introduces the
page and the Cannes/events sourcing of the interviewees directly on the
call.

**Alex, on why he started:** At the beginning, one of my biggest
frustrations that brought me to found AI Central was that, hey, I was
learning, but there was no path. It was a complete jungle out there. I
also started collecting resources, PDFs, tutorials, doing stuff myself,
and put everything in the early days in a Notion database that I was
sharing with friends. That feeling of learning about it but not finding
value - maybe the value was there, but it was scattered across the whole
internet, so full of friction. What people really need right now - more
information is all available to a certain degree - is to remove the
friction.

**Alex, on top mistakes people make with AI** (after Diraj's "they ask AI
what to build" and "being led by AI instead of leading AI"): Exactly...
AI is no judge of your situation, your context, and what is worth
implementing for you. The first thing I always recommend is map your
build, map your process. If you are not able to do that, don't jump into
automating with AI. If you are automating something crap, it is going to
automate and scale that crap. Manually, you'd make one mistake and correct
it. Once you automate something, once you make AI automate it, you may
wake up to something much worse.

[On tool selection] I'm an opinionated person, but AI is not for every use
case. If you want to solve something with AI, you may end up spending way
more than just using legacy tools, tools that are a bit more traditional.
There is still a lot of space for deterministic automation first - that
should be your foundations, deterministic stuff first, and then, not at a
later stage but after having covered the foundations, moving into using
AI to do the things deterministic tools can't. Using AI to parse a JSON
doesn't make any sense - you may end up with hallucinations. If there is a
deterministic rule, regex, some other type of deterministic rule, it
should always be favored: A, because it's cheaper; B, because you can
control the result way, way better.

[On organizational AI adoption, in response to Diraj's executives/seniors/
juniors framing] Senior-most executives should be challenging AI adoption
- not blocking it, backing it up, but challenging it: what does one run
cost me, what is the unit economics of putting this in place, what happens
if it goes down at 2am, who is accountable for the output. Mid-layer
management's job is mainly to review what is being produced from AI
rather than implement it - juniors are the ones building it, but seniors
translate whatever AI is producing before passing it up. So: AI as a
thinking partner plus reviewer at the senior layer, AI as a producer at
the junior layer. And all three should always think about the
non-functional requirements - security, cost, performance. It's not going
to be about how many AI agents I have deployed. It will be more about how
better organized, how better governed AI is in my organization.

[Adding to this, on his own consultancy work] At AI Central Media we are
both a media company and we run a small boutique consultancy for SMEs. The
approach for executives should be focused obsessively on ROI - two
dimensions, the dollar value and the horizon in which you can see the
first results. Seniors should be mapping out where the leaky bucket is -
where is my team spending the most, what are the bottlenecks - and
evaluating providers, deciding on the stack, in conjunction with the
people that will actually use it. This is why we should have AI champions
inside each company, which are the juniors. Otherwise it always feels like
a soccer game, eleven people on the one ball, without a proper way of
segmenting the decisions.

[On newsletters vs. courses] Courses are true to a timestamp. A newsletter,
I can keep on teaching whatever is happening with the latest [AI] world -
things change at such a fast rate, within three months a lot of things
change, get obsolete. [Example: a Nano Banana course going stale as the
model line evolves to Nano Banana Pro, then ChatGPT's own image
generation moves past both.] If you structure your course around something
foundational - non-functional requirements of software engineering that
have lasted 50 years, in one waveform or another - you can nail a course
that holds for the next 5-10 years. But most AI-topic courses won't live
past a timestamp. With a newsletter, the continuity is there - I can keep
adding to the lessons, keep editing. And someone is sending you that -
you can reply to it, talk to that person. On YouTube, fewer people reach
out than on a newsletter.

[On workspace philosophy] The problem we try to fix, doing bespoke
advisory, is teaching people archetypical, fundamental knowledge instead
of tool-specific knowledge - hedging the tool risk. If you want to do
image generation and prompting, it's better from a creator point of view
to build a course around how to prompt for images generally - JSON
prompting, colors, how to think about any image-generation model - than
around one specific tool, which is stale in three months. From a learner
perspective: don't follow the tool, follow the logic behind it. Whether
it's n8n, Make, or Zapier, you want to automate the process - the process
you end up having is very similar. You've got to learn the archetypical
knowledge, then map it onto whichever module the tool you're using has.

We are probably at a point where the baseline AI model is already good at
doing maybe ten use cases. [iPhone analogy, echoing the Michael Simmons
interview] I have an iPhone 13 right now, even though there's a 17th.
Still takes good pictures - for my usage, which is minimal, I don't have to
shoot 4K video. For me, the normal thing works.

Among AI Central readers, if I were to be honest, the number one question
is: should they be using ChatGPT or Claude or Copilot? I don't have the
answer - I'm not an expert in that field, in the sense that it doesn't
matter which. Choose whatever you want, as long as your workspace is set
up correctly - your memory set up correctly, your context set up
correctly, your skills reviewed as you move through. Personalization of
your workspace has an outsized value now that the brains are of similar
value to each other. No one is teaching people how to set up their
workspace, and this is one of the things I'm concentrating on the most -
your AI workspace should reflect your way of operating, not the other
way around. Otherwise you keep passing context, and you end up
suffocating in that AI slop.

[Closing, his own three takeaways] AI cannot be a shortcut for not having
foundational knowledge - invest in learning concepts, not tools. And
third: make a decision, stick with it, especially on your stack - find
the one use case you use every day that can actually improve your
productivity, even if it's 1%, if you keep using it every day.
